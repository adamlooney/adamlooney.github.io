> **Draft proposal for discussion.** This is a pitch for an alternative approach to ECON 1740, not a course that currently exists. Comments welcome: [alooney@gmail.com](mailto:alooney@gmail.com).

## The opportunity

ECON 1740 is the **only economics course that satisfies the General Education American Institutions requirement** — one of a handful of courses every Utah undergraduate can use to meet a state-mandated requirement. That makes it the economics department's single best shot at reaching students who would never otherwise take an economics class: a recruiting front door for the major, at scale.

The current course is a fully asynchronous online offering organized around documentary videos and survey history. It serves students, but it doesn't do what this seat in the curriculum could do: **show thousands of non-majors what modern economics actually is** — charts, causal questions, live policy debates — and why they might want more of it.

This page sketches an alternative: a design that one instructor could teach, or that several faculty could divide, adapt, and improve. Everything described below already exists in draft — a syllabus, a one-page sheet for each of the 25 classes, full 75-minute teaching notes, and a compiled deck of 68 charts. Downloads are at the [bottom of the page](#the-materials).

## The design in one sentence

> **Every class is built around one killer chart, one economic idea, and one American institution — and the 25 classes tell one continuous story: how America built a growth machine out of institutions, who got access to it, and how it governs capitalism's consequences.**

Three design rules make it work:

**1. Charts before theories.** Each lecture is anchored in a single compelling chart — the kind that stops a room. Students learn to interrogate each one: *What's the comparison? What's the counterfactual? How could this chart mislead me?* That habit — not any list of dates — is what modern economics is.

**2. History paired with the present.** Every historical episode has a live modern twin: railroads and broadband, the 1924 quotas and the 2021 immigration surge, Standard Oil and Big Tech, the Panic of 1907 and the 2023 bank failures. Students never have to ask why the material matters.

**3. One argument, four acts.** The course is a single narrative, not 25 topics. **Part I — The Growth Miracle** (why growth happened, and why in the Anglo-American world). **Part II — Building the American Growth Machine** (markets, invention, firms, wages). **Part III — Mobilizing People and Expanding Opportunity** (who got access: labor, education, immigration, women, race, place, mobility). **Part IV — Governing Capitalism's Consequences** — the government's toolkit: rules, provision, insurance, stabilization, and pricing harms, bookended by globalization and the AI future.

The framing is unapologetically positive about American institutions and market capitalism — and honest about failures, because the failures (slavery, exclusion, panics, pollution) are precisely where the economics is most instructive. Religious liberty runs through the course as a real economic institution, not a footnote — and Utah examples (the Golden Spike at Promontory Summit, LDS immigration, the Right to Work law, Salt Lake housing costs, the Opportunity Atlas for Salt Lake County) give the material local punch.

## The American Institutions requirement

Utah Code 53B-16-103 requires instruction in **the history, principles, form of government, and economic system of the United States.** The redesign covers all three prongs, from an economic lens:

| Statutory prong | Where it lives |
| --- | --- |
| **Economic system** | Every class — the system of free enterprise *is* the subject. |
| **American history** | The evidence base of every class: founding-era finance (5, 22), slavery and the Civil War (11), industrialization (7–10), the Great Migration (16), the Depression (23), the postwar boom (10, 13), globalization (19). |
| **Principles & form of government** | A dedicated class on the Constitution as an economic document (5), plus the Bill of Rights and religious liberty (4–5), the 13th–15th Amendments (11, 16), federalism (12, 17, 21), the taxing power and Social Security Act (22), the Federal Reserve Act (23), and the Sherman Act (20). |

Essential Learning Outcomes are met through chart and data interpretation, short primary-source readings (Magna Carta, Locke, the First Amendment, the 13th Amendment, Hamilton's *Report on Public Credit*, the Sherman Act), structured debates with assigned sides, and policy-memo writing assignments.

## Why the department wins

- **A recruiting pipeline.** Thousands of gen-ed students see real modern economics — Goldin, Chetty, Autor, the 2024 Nobel — taught through questions they already care about (housing costs, student debt, AI, immigration). The course plants the major explicitly.
- **A template, not a personality.** Because every class has the same architecture (chart → idea → institution → debate), any faculty member can pick up a class, swap in their own research, or teach a guest lecture. The one-page class sheets are designed to be handed off.
- **Scales.** One instructor in a large lecture; selected classes suit guest lecturers whose research matches.
- **Low startup cost.** The materials below already exist in draft, along with scripts that rebuild every chart from public data.
